# Ogun Axe Reference

## Summary

Primary museum records point to a simple rule: Ogun is marked by a single axe, sword, staff, or other iron tool, while Shango is marked by a double axe. For a small SIGIDI logo, the safest choice is a minimal single-blade mark with a short shaft and no twin heads, no double blade, and no figure-heavy crown.

## Verified Evidence

- UMMA states that a single axe is associated with Ogun and a double axe is associated with Shango. Source: https://umma.umich.edu/objects/ceremonial-axe-1998-1-53/
- UMMA describes an `Ax for Ogun` as a ceremonial ax with a carved handle and a triangular iron blade embedded in the top. UMMA also notes that Ogun is dear to people whose work depends on iron and that Ogun is embodied in the sharpened edge of iron blades. Source: https://umma.umich.edu/objects/ax-for-ogun-2006-2-73/
- UMMA describes an `ada Ogun` ceremonial sword with a short cylindrical handle wrapped with brass rings and a large flat blade that curves upward. Source: https://umma.umich.edu/objects/ceremonial-sword-1998-1-64/
- High Museum describes `Blacksmith's Staff (Opa Ogun)` as ceremonial, for a senior blacksmith and possibly a hunter or warrior, with bird motifs and projections that signal power linked to Ogun. Source: https://high.org/collection/blacksmiths-staff-opa-ogun/
- The Detroit Institute of Arts describes `Shango Wand` as a distinctive double-bladed wooden staff that publicly identifies Shango worshippers, and says the double-axe motif expresses Shango's nature. Source: https://dia.org/collection/shango-wand/65728
- The Detroit Institute of Arts also describes a Shango cult object whose headdress is shaped like a double-bladed axe carried by two outward-facing heads. Source: https://dia.org/collection/twin-figure-shango-cult/97843
- The British Museum records a ritual sword of the Ogun cult as brass, cast and hammered, with a pierced blade and punched ornament. Source: https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/E_Af1887-0808-2
- The British Museum records sixteen miniature blacksmith tools strung on an iron wire as ritual objects in the cult of Ogun. Source: https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/E_Af1962-17-37?selectedImageId=1613383674

## Design Inference

- A small logo should use one blade only. That keeps the mark in Ogun territory and avoids Shango's double-axe form. Inference from: https://umma.umich.edu/objects/ceremonial-axe-1998-1-53/ and https://dia.org/collection/shango-wand/65728
- The best small-size silhouette is a short shaft with one narrow triangular blade. That shape is still legible at low size. Inference from: https://umma.umich.edu/objects/ax-for-ogun-2006-2-73/ and https://umma.umich.edu/objects/ceremonial-sword-1998-1-64/
- The logo should avoid twin heads, paired blades, crown-like heads, and thunderbolt-like branching. Those details read as Shango rather than Ogun. Inference from: https://dia.org/collection/twin-figure-shango-cult/97843 and https://dia.org/collection/shango-wand/65728
- The logo should also avoid figure-heavy handles. UMMA and High Museum examples use carved figures and birds, but those details will collapse at icon size. Inference from: https://umma.umich.edu/objects/ceremonial-axe-1998-1-53/ and https://high.org/collection/blacksmiths-staff-opa-ogun/

## Recommendation

Use a minimal single axe mark for SIGIDI. Make the blade narrow and geometric. Keep the shaft short. Remove human figures, birds, rings, and ornament. Do not use a double axe. If the final mark needs cultural sign-off, review it with a Yoruba cultural adviser before release.

## Notes

This report separates object records from design judgment. The design choice is an inference, not a museum claim.
